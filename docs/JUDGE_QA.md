# Judge Q&A — twelve likely questions

Short, direct answers with the evidence you can point at on screen or in the repository.
Numbers match the seeded dataset (`npm run validate`).

---

### 1. "Is any of this real data?"

No — and the UI says so on every page. The 15 farmers, 8 buyers, 20 towns, market prices and
90-day histories are generated by `backend/scripts/generate-data.ts` with a fixed seed (42).
The demo date is 15 November 2026. What is real is the pipeline: constraints, scoring,
allocators, anomaly detection, API validation, tests and the UI all run on that data exactly
as they would on a real feed.

If pressed: "The honest claim is that the *methods* are production-shaped and the *data* is
simulated. Swapping the loader for a database and a price feed would not change the domain
logic."

---

### 2. "Why those scoring weights, and why that price formula?"

Weights are 30% price, 25% distance, 20% quantity fit, 15% quality, 10% availability. They are
a stated policy choice, not a discovered truth: price and logistics dominate a perishable
supply chain, quality and timing are secondary. They live in `config/weights.json`, are
exposed as sliders, and are renormalised server-side, so the policy can be changed without a
code change.

The price component is anchored on the market reference:
`100 · clamp(0.5 + (reference − asking) / (0.4 · reference))`. Paying the reference scores 50;
20% below scores 100; 20% above scores 0. This makes "fair market price" the neutral point
rather than treating the cheapest offer as automatically best.

The important property is not the exact numbers — it is that every component is bounded 0–100,
so the weighted total is a genuine 0–100 score, and every component is shown to the user.

---

### 3. "How do I know the explanation isn't just decoration?"

Explanations are generated from the same `PairScore` object that produced the ranking
(`backend/src/domain/explain.ts`). They name the strongest and weakest components with their
values, quote the actual prices and distances, and state the binding constraint for rejected
pairs. If the anomaly rule changed the reference or halved the price weight, the explanation
says so.

There is no language model involved, so it cannot hallucinate a reason that the numbers do not
support. The drawer in Matching and the allocation detail show the score components next to
the prose — you can verify the sentence against the bars.

---

### 4. "Why three allocation strategies? Which one is best?"

Because "best" depends on the policy objective, and the brief asks the system to expose that
trade-off rather than hide it.

- **Greedy by score** — simplest and fastest; takes the best pair first. Can starve small
  buyers.
- **Min-cost max-flow** — maximises total kilograms first, then minimises score loss plus
  freight. Best when volume and cost dominate.
- **Fairness-weighted** — protects low-fill buyers; raises Jain's index at a small cost in
  average suitability.

On scenario A all three move roughly 14.5–14.7 t, so the decision is about *who* is served.
On scenario B, where only 9.0 t can move against 15.5 t of demand, the difference in fairness
is the point. The Strategies page shows the badges and the measured trade-offs for the current
run.

---

### 5. "Is the min-cost max-flow solver actually correct, or did you just call a library?"

It is written from scratch in `backend/src/allocation/minCostFlow.ts` — no LP package in
production code. The graph is source → farmers → buyers → sink. Successive shortest
augmenting paths are found with SPFA (which handles the negative residual arcs created by
earlier augmentations), and flow is pushed until no path remains.

Capacities are integers, so the result is integral: no fractional kilograms. Costs are integer
milli-rupees, so there is no floating-point comparison in the solver.

Correctness evidence: `tests/solver.test.ts` compares the solver's objective with
`javascript-lp-solver` on generated instances (the LP package is a dev dependency used only as
a test oracle), and checks conservation and capacity on every result. The allocation suite
also asserts that no farmer ships more than they have and no buyer receives more than they
asked for.

The one modelling caveat is minimum lots: a pure flow network cannot express "nothing or at
least L kg". A visible repair pass drops sub-lot deliveries and reallocates the kilograms,
reporting how many repairs it made.

---

### 6. "Is the fairness allocator optimal?"

No, and the UI says so. It is a concave-welfare greedy heuristic: each step gives the next
10 kg quantum to the pair maximising `score × (1 − buyerFill)²`. That strongly favours
under-served buyers, but it does not prove the max-min optimum.

We chose this deliberately: every step is auditable, and the outcome is measured with Jain's
index rather than asserted. On scenario A it moves 14.52 t instead of 14.70 t while raising
Jain from 0.9844 to 0.9908 — a real, measurable trade-off. If a judge wants exact max-min, the
correct next step is an LP with explicit fairness constraints, not a stronger claim about this
heuristic.

---

### 7. "What does Jain's index add that fill rate doesn't?"

Fill rate is an average; it can hide starvation. Jain's index
`J = (Σxᵢ)² / (n·Σxᵢ²)` measures how evenly fill is distributed across buyers: 1.0 means every
buyer is equally served, lower values mean concentration. Scenario A has 94.8% fill and
J = 0.9844 (very even). Scenario B has 58.1% fill and J = 0.9156 — the shortage is not spread
evenly. Reporting both prevents "we moved a lot of kilograms" from masking "three buyers got
nothing".

---

### 8. "Why 250 km, and why draw straight lines on the map if the distance is haversine × 1.3?"

The 250 km cap is a policy parameter in `config/constraints.json`, not a physical law. It
represents a perishable-logistics limit: beyond roughly a day's refrigerated road travel, the
trade is not viable. Road distance is approximated as great-circle distance × 1.3, a standard
circuity factor for Sri Lankan terrain; the true value would need a road graph.

The map is explicitly illustrative. It plots the simulated towns and draws straight legs so
you can see the corridor pattern — including the blocked Badulla–Colombo route in scenario B.
The line is not a route, and both the map legend and the README say the score uses the
haversine approximation.

---

### 9. "How does the anomaly detector work, and why cap its influence?"

For each product, take the previous 14 days (excluding today), compute mean and standard
deviation, and form `z = (price − mean) / σ`. Flag at `|z| ≥ 3`. The robust reference is the
30-day median.

Scenario B's tomato jumps to Rs 184/kg against a trailing mean near Rs 123/kg, giving
z ≈ 13.7; scenario A's tomato is Rs 127 and z ≈ 0.1. Non-tomato series are identical across
scenarios, so none has a current flag — that is the control.

When flagged, scoring uses the median (Rs 127 here) and halves the price weight, redistributing
the rest. This prevents one bad tick from reordering every match. The catch is stated in the
UI: capping influence means a genuine permanent shift would be treated as anomalous for as
long as the old baseline dominates the window. A production system would add change-point
detection and human review.

---

### 10. "Why TanStack Query and Zustand together? Isn't that redundant?"

They hold different things. TanStack Query owns **server state**: fetching, caching, retries,
invalidation, and refetch-on-key-change. Every key includes the scenario id (and weights or
strategy where relevant), so switching scenario automatically refetches the right data without
manual wiring. Zustand owns **local UI state**: selected scenario, allocation strategy,
shortage policy, scoring weights. It stores no API payloads, so there is no second cache to
keep consistent.

All HTTP goes through one axios client (`shared/services/api-client.ts`), which normalises
errors and sets the base URL. Mutations invalidate the relevant query families in one place
(`shared/services/invalidate.ts`).

---

### 11. "How do you know it works, and how fast is it?"

- **76 backend tests across 7 files** (`npm test`): geo, scoring, anomaly, allocation, solver
  (against an LP oracle), data integrity, and 25 API tests covering status codes, validation,
  aliases, caching, dashboard shapes and CSV upload.
- **`npm run validate`** asserts conservation (allocated ≤ supply, ≤ demand), minimum lots,
  capacity limits, scenario-B derivation and the expected anomaly pattern, then prints timings.
- **Cold compute** for a full scenario allocation is roughly **1–5 ms** at this size; identical
  requests are served from a 60 s in-process cache keyed by data revision and parameters.
- Frontend: `tsc --noEmit` clean and a successful production build with lazy per-page chunks.

The honest caveat: the instance is 15 farmers × 8 buyers. The algorithms are polynomial, but
this implementation has not been load-tested at thousands of nodes. At larger scale the
caching strategy and the SPFA solver would need revisiting (e.g. potentials/Dijkstra, or an
off-the-shelf min-cost-flow implementation).

---

### 12. "What would it take to put this into production?"

In rough order:

1. **Persistence and concurrency** — replace the in-memory store with a database and
   transactions; CSV import and CRUD currently reset on restart.
2. **Real identity and authorisation** — farmers, buyers and a coordinator need separate roles
   and audit trails; the prototype has none.
3. **Real prices and routing** — a market feed with timestamps, currency/unit normalisation,
   and a road-network distance service instead of haversine × 1.3.
4. **Model governance** — weights, thresholds and the 250 km cap should be versioned policy
   with sign-off; the anomaly cap should include change-point detection and human review.
5. **Scale and observability** — database-backed caching, metrics/tracing, and load tests on
   realistic graph sizes.
6. **Fairness guarantees** — if fairness is a regulatory requirement, move from the heuristic
   to an explicit constrained optimisation with an auditable objective.

The prototype is deliberately honest about these gaps: they are listed in the README and in
the strategies/prices pages, not hidden behind the demo.
